P1DRAFT

Governance & Risk Oversight

PRODUCTION CONTINUITY · ROI-GATED INVESTMENT · RANSOMWARE READINESS

The foundation pillar — but in manufacturing, honest governance starts by admitting where budget actually comes from: production continuity, contractual pressure, and fines, not risk appetite alone. Draft v0.1, benchmarked against IEC 62443.

Establish joint OT/IT Security Governance with plant-manager and IT leadership sharing accountability

IEC 62443-1

Define risk appetite explicitly in terms of production-downtime cost and regulatory fine exposure — document the real ROI-gated investment pattern rather than an aspirational risk-based one

◆ RICE-MFG

The core honesty objective — most Malaysian manufacturers only invest in security when it protects revenue or avoids a fine, the same adoption pattern seen globally with AI investment. No existing framework designs governance around this reality instead of assuming risk-based maturity from day one.

Mandate a ransomware-specific incident response plan with OT/IT network isolation procedures to contain blast radius

IEC 62443-3◆ RICE-MFG

Ransomware-first framing — treated as the practical entry point since it's the one attack type guaranteed to stop the production line, which is what actually earns budget approval

Conduct an honest minimum-viable security baseline assessment — calibrate RICE maturity scoring to a realistic L1 starting point, with a defined path to L2/L3 tied to measurable triggers

◆ RICE-MFG

RICE Maturity Model, deliberately anchored to where plants actually are rather than where FSI/Healthcare start — pretending otherwise makes the framework unusable on day one

Map cyber-insurance requirements and OEM customer contract security clauses as the practical governance forcing-function

◆ RICE-MFG

Regulatory and ethical framing alone doesn't move plant-floor budget — insurers and customer contracts increasingly do, and RICE-MFG treats that as a legitimate lever rather than ignoring it

Assess Cybersecurity Act 2024 (Act 854) exposure — direct obligations where designated, indirect obligations where supplying an NCII-sector customer

Cybersecurity Act 2024 (Act 854)

Establish a complete OT asset inventory as the governance prerequisite — most plants don't have an accurate picture of what's actually connected

IEC 62443-2

Address plant-management and shift-leadership rotation risk — maintain institutional OT security memory across plant-manager and engineering-lead transitions

◆ RICE-MFG

Rotation-resilient governance, adapted from RICE-FSI P1.08 — plant leadership turnover carries the same institutional-memory risk, often with less documentation to fall back on

Key Controls (draft)

  • Documented risk appetite explicitly tied to downtime cost and fine exposure, not abstract risk scoring
  • Ransomware-specific incident response plan with OT/IT isolation runbook
  • RICE-MFG Maturity Model scoring anchored to a realistic L1 baseline
  • Cyber-insurance and customer-contract security requirement register
  • Complete OT asset inventory, reviewed at minimum annually

What RICE-MFG benchmarks against (draft)

  • IEC 62443 — zones and conduits, security levels, OT/ICS-specific controls
  • Cybersecurity Act 2024 (Act 854) — indirect exposure via NCII-sector customers
  • Real Malaysian manufacturing adoption pattern — documented, not assumed
P2DRAFT

Threat Detection & Intelligence

OT-AWARE MONITORING · RANSOMWARE PRECURSORS · ICS-CERT INTEL

Draft — detection tuned for OT behavior, not IT anomaly signatures applied where they don't fit.

Deploy OT-aware intrusion detection, distinct from generic IT monitoring tooling

Monitor for ransomware pre-encryption indicators — unusual file enumeration, backup deletion attempts

◆ RICE

Pre-encryption detection is the highest-leverage control available — catching the precursor beats responding to the encryption event

Establish a baseline of normal OT network behaviour before deploying anomaly detection

Integrate threat intelligence feeds specific to ICS/SCADA vulnerabilities — ICS-CERT advisories

Monitor IIoT device traffic for anomalous outbound connections

P3DRAFT

Identity & Access Governance

OT ROLE-BASED ACCESS · SHARED TERMINALS · REMOTE VENDOR MFA

Draft — access governance accounts for how the plant floor actually operates, not how an office does.

Implement OT-specific role-based access distinct from IT RBAC, per the IEC 62443 role model

IEC 62443-3

Address shared-terminal reality on the production floor — session timeout and re-authentication rather than assuming one user per device

◆ RICE

Plant-floor identity is genuinely different: a workstation is often shared across a shift, which most access frameworks don't design for

Enforce MFA for remote access to OT networks — vendor and integrator remote support is a common entry point

◆ RICE

Remote-support access is one of the most common real-world OT intrusion paths and often the least monitored

Time-bound OEM and integrator remote access, disabled by default outside maintenance windows

Conduct periodic access review for engineering workstation and HMI access

P4DRAFT

Incident Response & Resilience

RANSOMWARE CONTAINMENT · MANUAL FALLBACK · DOWNTIME-WEIGHTED RTO

Draft — response planning centres on keeping the line running, or restarting it fast, since that's what governance actually optimises for here.

Define a ransomware containment procedure that isolates OT from IT without halting the entire plant where possible

◆ RICE

Selective isolation, not an all-or-nothing shutdown — the goal is containing blast radius while preserving as much production as safely possible

Establish manual or offline fallback procedures for critical production processes

Conduct an annual ransomware tabletop exercise involving plant operations, not just IT

Define RTO per production line, weighted explicitly by revenue impact per hour of downtime

◆ RICE

Directly operationalises P1.02's honesty principle — recovery targets stated in the currency that actually drives plant-floor decisions

Maintain offline, tested backups of OT configuration and historian data

P5DRAFT

Data Security & Privacy

PROCESS IP PROTECTION · HISTORIAN ENCRYPTION · RECIPE DATA

Draft — the sensitive asset here is process and product IP first, personal data second.

Protect proprietary process data and manufacturing IP as the primary data-security concern, not personal data

◆ RICE

A framing shift from most data-security guidance, which defaults to personal-data protection — for a plant, the recipe and process parameters are the crown jewels

Encrypt data in transit between the OT historian and any cloud or analytics platform

Classify and restrict access to product formulation, recipe, and process-parameter data

Apply baseline data protection to personal data collected — HR records, visitor logs — per PDPA

Define data retention for production and quality records per industry and customer contract requirements

P6DRAFT

Supply Chain & Third-Party Risk

OEM ACCESS TIERING · CONTRACT SECURITY CLAUSES · NACSA LICENSING

Draft — the OEM and integrator relationship is the primary third-party risk, more than generic vendor management.

Inventory and tier OEM and integrator remote-access relationships to OT networks

◆ RICE

The starting point most plants skip — you can't govern access you haven't inventoried

Require security requirements in vendor contracts as a condition of OT network access

Verify NACSA licensing status for any cybersecurity service providers engaged

Assess fourth-party risk for cloud-connected industrial equipment — your vendor's vendor

Map customer-contract security requirements as a governance driver

◆ RICE

Ties to P1.05 — increasingly it's the OEM customer's contract, not regulation, that sets the real security bar

P7DRAFT

Infrastructure Best Practices

IEC 62443 ZONES & CONDUITS · PURDUE DMZ · LEGACY PLC HARDENING

Draft — segmentation is the single highest-leverage infrastructure control available to a plant that's starting from a low baseline.

Implement the IEC 62443 zones-and-conduits model to segment OT from IT

IEC 62443-3

Establish an Industrial DMZ — Purdue Model Level 3.5 — as the mandatory buffer zone between OT (Levels 0–3) and IT (Levels 4–5)

Purdue Model L3.5

Assign explicit, named ownership of the Level 3.5 DMZ — patching, monitoring, and change control — resolving the IT/OT handoff before an incident forces the question

◆ RICE

The DMZ is where responsibility most often falls through the crack — IT assumes OT owns it because it touches the plant floor, OT assumes IT owns it because it's "just a firewall." RICE-MFG requires a single named owner and a documented escalation path, reviewed at least annually as the boundary shifts with OT/IT convergence.

Log and monitor all Level 3.5 DMZ traffic bidirectionally, treating the boundary itself as a detection point, not just a control point

◆ RICE

A firewall rule set without traffic monitoring only tells you what should happen, not what did — the DMZ is one of the highest-value places in the whole network to actually watch

Harden legacy PLCs and SCADA systems that cannot be patched, via compensating network controls

◆ RICE

Compensating controls are the realistic answer for equipment with a 15-20 year service life that was never designed to be patched

Define a patch management process that accounts for production-window constraints

Maintain physical security controls for OT equipment access — cabinet locks, port controls

P8DRAFT

Secure Application Ecosystem

SCADA/MES/HMI HARDENING · VENDOR SOFTWARE VETTING · DEFAULT CREDENTIALS

Draft — the application layer here is industrial software, with its own default-credential and legacy-vulnerability problems.

Secure SCADA/MES/HMI application access and configuration change control

Apply secure SDLC to any custom manufacturing execution system development

Test vendor-supplied industrial software for known vulnerabilities before deployment

◆ RICE

Pre-deployment vetting — a surprising amount of industrial software ships with known, unpatched vulnerabilities baked in

Harden any cloud-connected production-monitoring dashboards against unauthorized access

Review default credentials on all newly commissioned OT equipment before go-live

◆ RICE

The single most common OT compromise vector globally, and the cheapest one to close

P9DRAFT

Emerging Threat Horizon

SMART MANUFACTURING · LIGHTS-OUT PRODUCTION · IIOT ATTACK SURFACE

Draft — Industry 4.0 connectivity is expanding the attack surface faster than most plants' governance is maturing to match, and the next step beyond it — lights-out, near-fully-autonomous production — removes the one thing that used to catch problems by accident: a person on the floor.

Assess attack surface growth from IIoT sensor proliferation on the plant floor

◆ RICE

Every new sensor is a new endpoint — inventory and risk assessment need to keep pace with Industry 4.0 rollout, not trail it

Evaluate AI-driven predictive maintenance tools for data-integrity and manipulation risk

Monitor supply-chain-embedded firmware risk in industrial equipment

Begin planning for OT/IT convergence risk as Industry 4.0 initiatives expand connectivity

Assess ransomware-as-a-service targeting patterns specific to the manufacturing sector

Scale telemetry and monitoring coverage in direct proportion to production autonomy — treat reduced human floor presence as a mandate to increase visibility, not a reason it went unnoticed

◆ RICE

The core lights-out-manufacturing principle — a human walking the floor used to catch smoke, sound, or a stopped line by accident. Remove that person and the monitoring system has to catch everything a human incidentally would have. No existing framework states this as an explicit design requirement.

Define automated contingency and fail-safe procedures for unmanned or minimally-manned production runs — safe shutdown, containment, and alerting without waiting for human intervention

◆ RICE

In a fully-staffed plant, a person can physically intervene within minutes. In a lights-out cell, the system itself has to be the first responder — this has to be engineered in advance, not improvised during an incident

Assess cascading-failure risk unique to highly-autonomous lines, where one compromised control loop can propagate faster than any human-paced response could contain

◆ RICE

Autonomy raises both the speed of legitimate production and the speed at which an attack or fault can cascade — response design has to assume machine-speed events, not human-speed ones
